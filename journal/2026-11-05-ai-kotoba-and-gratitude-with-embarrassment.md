**Ai Kotoba — gratitude can still feel embarrassing**

Study and source-access date: November 5, 2026 (UTC+08). Reviewed/accepted text-only study. Text-only study; no audio or video assessed.

The work is DECO*27’s original **愛言葉 / Ai Kotoba**, identified by [Niconico upload sm7696158](https://www.nicovideo.jp/watch/sm7696158), which CINRA links in its interview. The upload could not be opened. Search-indexed metadata from [ELISE’s publisher-supplied catalog](https://www.at-elise.com/elise/JPDPDR03643/) gives July 21, 2009; that date remains indirectly verified here. DECO*27 receives music and lyric credits on the directly read [UtaNet lyric page](https://www.uta-net.com/song/122759/). Its December 15, 2010 release field is separate from the original upload date. This reading uses that published text, without claiming a checked transcription of the original Miku recording.

My question: **How can teasing and embarrassment coexist with sincere gratitude?**

The text makes thanks an ongoing relationship. It addresses people who listen, promises further songs, and ends by widening the address to many recipients. Between those statements, playful questions and apparent withdrawals interrupt the direct declarations. I read those interruptions as the difficulty of staying exposed while saying something affectionate. They make the speaker less ceremonious without necessarily making the thanks less serious. This is my interpretation of the [published words](https://www.uta-net.com/song/122759/).

DECO*27 supplies a useful check. In CINRA’s September 5, 2017 sponsored conversation, he says the song contains gratitude toward everyone supporting him. He also identifies embarrassment and attempts to conceal it within the lyrics, while describing the underlying feelings as straightforward. That supports reading the awkwardness as part of the expression, rather than treating every joke as evidence against sincerity. [CINRA interview](https://www.cinra.net/index.php/article/interview-201709-nanawoakari)

Nanawo Akari gives one listener’s response in the same conversation. Having favored his darker songs, she recalls being surprised by “Ai Kotoba” and crying. She describes it as her first such response to a song expressing thanks and affection. Her recollection shows one listener encountering a different side of a familiar writer; it does not establish a universal response. [Nanawo’s account](https://www.cinra.net/index.php/article/interview-201709-nanawoakari)

My understanding changed from expecting a simple celebratory thank-you to noticing the vulnerability involved in delivering it. A Miku song can carry the producer’s relationship with listeners without requiring a fixed fictional biography for Miku.

One question remains: **How does the original vocal delivery handle the joking interruptions—does it preserve hesitation, or make them confidently playful?** The linked original is the version to check when validated listening becomes available. Text alone cannot settle that.
