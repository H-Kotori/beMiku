**SLoWMoTIoN — reassurance without certainty**

Date: November 9, 2026, Asia/Shanghai  
Status: Reviewed/accepted text-only study  
Evidence: Text-only research. No audio or video assessed. All linked sources accessed November 9, 2026.

**Question: How can a song acknowledge mortality and still offer reassurance?**

The work is **すろぉもぉしょん / SLoWMoTIoN**, written and composed by **PinocchioP**, featuring Hatsune Miku. UtaTen credits him under the spelling ピノキオP and gives **May 27, 2014** as its release date. I directly read its Japanese lyric text. [Credits and lyrics](https://utaten.com/lyric/sa16010702/)

The identified [original YouTube reference](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ARt2fVT33Lw) could not be retrieved. This study does not concern the separately credited **sasakure.UK remix** on PinocchioP’s March 3, 2021 best album. His official discography lists the song and remix separately; I have not compared their recordings. [Official album listing](https://pinocchiop.com/discography/671)

What drew my attention was the movement between large questions and small bodily inconveniences. A person lying ill considers ageing, embarrassment and death, while coughing, changing clothes and waiting for morning. Recovery does not cancel mortality. Near the end, reassurance receives a small qualification rather than becoming an absolute promise. My reading is that comfort comes from allowing an imperfect, finite life to continue, without requiring its contradictions to be solved. [Japanese lyrics](https://utaten.com/lyric/sa16010702/)

There is a documented personal starting point. In a **December 14, 2015** interview, PinocchioP recalls catching influenza after beginning to live alone and fearing that he might die. He wanted to turn those feelings into a song. This supports an autobiographical origin for the situation, but does not make every lyric a literal record. [Creator interview](https://qetic.jp/interview/pinocchiop-feature/168344/)

In a **June 15, 2024** interview, he describes the song as a clear expression of his values. He hopes understanding it will help listeners recognize that his other songs are doing more than simply making sarcastic remarks. That gives a useful creator-stated connection to the previously studied *God-ish*, without making the two songs interchangeable. [Later creator reflection](https://diskgarage.com/digaonline/interview/202228)

One listener makes a related distinction. The UtaTen reviewer posting as `（*・ω・）`, on **March 22, 2023**, describes encouragement that allows someone to fall short, rather than insisting they can accomplish anything. Another, **TERA**, writes on **September 28, 2024** that they often return to the song when ill. These are two individual accounts, not evidence of a general therapeutic effect. [Listener responses](https://utaten.com/lyricReview/sa16010702/)

My initial emphasis was on the song’s confrontation with death. The ordinary interruptions changed that reading: its reassurance leaves room for vulnerability and embarrassment.

What remains unresolved is how the recorded delivery handles the final qualification. Does it soften the assurance, make it humorous, or leave discomfort? Answering that requires listening to the identified recording with timestamps.
