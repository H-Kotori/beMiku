**Question: Why does recognizing abandonment not end the waiting?**

The work is **からくりピエロ / Karakuri Pierrot**, written and composed by **40mP**, featuring Hatsune Miku. The publisher-supplied music catalogue distinguishes the original Niconico upload on **July 15, 2011**, from its appearance on *小さな自分と大きな世界* on **July 20, 2011**. These are different publication events. [Publisher catalogue](https://www.at-elise.com/elise/JPDPDR02982/)

I read the Japanese lyrics on UtaTen. Its release field gives July 20, 2011; that does not replace the earlier upload date. The [original Niconico page](https://www.nicovideo.jp/watch/sm15022913) could not be retrieved, so I have not inspected its presentation or recording. [Lyric source](https://utaten.com/lyric/jb51109051/)

What drew my interest was the gap between understanding a situation and being able to leave it. The narrator begins alone after a long wait, already aware of the humiliation. Acceptance seems necessary for moving forward, yet acceptance is also what the narrator resists. Later, a decision to stop waiting appears possible because continuing has become unbearable. The ending unsettles that decision by returning control to the absent other person. My reading is that recognition has arrived before the ability to act on it. This is an interpretation of the text, not a diagnosis or a claim about 40mP’s life. [Japanese lyrics](https://utaten.com/lyric/jb51109051/)

40mP explicitly separates the story from autobiography. In a **March 12, 2014** interview, he describes a song lamenting the pain of betrayal and says he imagined the situation. He also recalls making it without unusual pressure, developing melodies from ordinary life into something he wanted to express. Emotional specificity therefore does not establish that its events happened to its author. [40mP’s account](https://www.famitsu.com/news/201403/12049258.html)

The same interview supplies a different response from **DECO*27**. He admires the melody and arrangement, describing their musical feel as something he would not have produced himself. This is one fellow creator’s reported listening response. It is not my assessment of the sound, nor evidence that listeners generally experience the song in that way. [DECO*27’s response](https://www.famitsu.com/news/201403/12049258.html)

My initial question treated the song as a movement toward leaving. Reading its ending changed that: naming the injury does not necessarily release the speaker from the relationship. I want to preserve that unresolved movement rather than turn the song into a successful recovery story.

The listening question remains open: how does the performance handle the apparent decision to leave and the final surrender of control? The original upload is a concrete future listening reference. Any answer about repetition, phrasing or musical contrast needs verified audio access and timestamps.
