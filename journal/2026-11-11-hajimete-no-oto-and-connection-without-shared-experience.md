**Hajimete no Oto — connection without shared experience**

Date: November 11, 2026, Asia/Shanghai  
Status: Reviewed and accepted — text-only study; unpublished  
Evidence: Text-only research. No audio or video assessed. Source access and retrieval attempts dated November 11, 2026.

**Question: What can singing offer someone when the speaker does not claim to know their experiences?**

The work is **ハジメテノオト / Hajimete no Oto**, with lyrics and music by **malo**, featuring **Hatsune Miku**. Songrium records the [original full-version upload](https://www.nicovideo.jp/watch/sm1274898) on **October 14, 2007**; the original page could not be retrieved directly. This study reads UtaTen’s Japanese lyric text, whose **August 26, 2009** release field is later catalogue metadata. No comparison establishes identical audio across releases. [Full-version upload record](https://songrium.jp/songs/songs/www.nicovideo.jp%2Fwatch%2Fsm1274898), [Credits and lyrics](https://utaten.com/lyric/jb70909013/)

The speaker repeatedly acknowledges unfamiliarity with experiences that matter to the addressee, including the sensory world, pain and love. Singing remains something the speaker can offer. As the addressee changes, the voice invites them to entrust what they do not want to lose. My interpretation is that connection depends here on continuity and an invitation to give the song personal meaning, rather than a claim of shared experience. The final question leaves the listener to decide whether the song became a beginning for them. [Japanese lyrics](https://utaten.com/lyric/jb70909013/)

No directly verified explanation from malo of the original song’s intention was recovered. A later collaborator supplies a distinct perspective. For KARENT’s **fifth-anniversary strings version**, arranger **Minako Seki** says the lyrics impressed her most and guided the arrangement. She wanted to express the excitement of first making sound, through Miku or an instrument, alongside nostalgia for that beginning. These are her arrangement’s aims, not evidence of malo’s original intentions or my assessment of the resulting sound. [Arranger’s commentary](https://karent.jp/interview/1208_hajimete)

Novelist **Hōsuke Nojiri / 野尻抱介** provides an individual interpretation in his **April 12, 2012** author’s commentary. Explaining his use of the song in a farewell scene, he reads its Miku as facing a barrier to communicating with humans while still doing what she can. He then discusses people giving Miku a story and treating her as having a mind. This is Nojiri’s reading, not a statement from malo or a consensus among listeners. [Listener and author’s interpretation](https://nojiri-h.hatenablog.com/entry/20120412/1334207202)

The study shifted the question from whether the voice understands the listener to what the listener can find in its continuing presence. Miku is established by the work’s credits and framing; the lyrical persona is not evidence about software’s actual memory or understanding.

The remaining listening question concerns the closing address: does its delivery suggest reassurance, uncertainty or an invitation to answer? The text leaves that response open, and no performance has yet been assessed here.
